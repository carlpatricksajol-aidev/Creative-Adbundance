# Ad Script Writer: Writing Rules

The craft file. Read this in full at Step 3, before drafting a single line. Everything here exists because a real client graded a batch of AI-written scripts and marked the same four things down: proof that did not match the promise, an offer parked in the last sentence, identical product introductions, and three hooks that were one thought reworded.

**Notation used in this file.** `[PRODUCT]` and `[BRAND]` are placeholders you replace with the real product name. All example personas (personal trainer, wedding photographer, first-time founder, bookkeeper, mobile groomer) are invented stand-ins. Never lift an example line into a client deliverable verbatim; lift the *construction*.

**The rule above all rules.** Every line you write serves the concept contract from Step 2: "This ad tests whether [PRODUCT] can [SPECIFIC PROMISE] for [PERSONA], proven by [MEASURABLE OUTCOME]." The hook opens that argument. The proof closes that exact argument. If a line is true and well written but belongs to a different contract, it is a defect. Cut it.

**House copy rule.** No em dashes anywhere in generated copy. Use commas, colons, periods, parentheses.

---

## 1. The Hook System

### 1.1 What a hook is

A hook is three seconds of a specific person's specific situation. It is not a claim, not a category statement, and not a question with an obvious answer. Each script ships **three hooks**, and each hook is an **entry point**: a different door into the same contract.

Every hook has two parts and is labelled:

```
Hook 1: Empty Launch / Pain Point
Overlay: 11 orders. That was the whole launch.
Opening Line: "I spent four months making this and eleven people bought it."
```

### 1.2 The labelling convention

`Hook N: [Situation Name] / [Angle Type]`

- **Situation Name** is 2 to 4 words naming the scene, not the theme. "Empty Launch", "February Silence", "Ninth Cold DM", "Referral Drought". Not "Growth Problem" or "Awareness Issue".
- **Angle Type** comes from the grid below and states *why this door is different*.

The label is a working promise to the reviewer. If the strategist cannot tell from the three labels alone that the hooks are different, the labels are wrong or the hooks are.

### 1.3 The angle grid: pick three rows, never three from one row

| Angle Type | The door it opens | Opening move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain Point | The cost being paid right now | Name the bad number or the bad moment |
| Goal / Aspiration | The outcome wanted, stated plainly | Name the thing they are trying to get to |
| Root Cause | The wrong diagnosis they have been treating | Correct the diagnosis in the first line |
| Persona Shift | Same product, different person entirely | Lead with the unexpected persona |
| Objection | The reason they have not tried this | Voice the objection as their own line |
| Cost of Inaction | What another quarter of this costs | Put a price or a count on waiting |
| Timing / Season | Why now and not later | Anchor to a date, season, or deadline |
| Status Quo Critique | The default method everyone uses is the problem | Attack the method, not the person |
| Comparison | The old channel versus this one | Two numbers, side by side |

**Selection rule.** Three hooks, three different rows. A Pain Point hook plus a Root Cause hook plus a Persona Shift hook is a legal set. Three Pain Point hooks wearing different sentences is an instant review failure.

**Contract discipline.** All three doors open into the same room. A hook that promises a different measurable outcome than the contract proves is a defect even if it is the strongest line in the draft. Rewrite the hook, do not rewrite the contract.

### 1.4 The swap test for hooks

Take Hook 1's opening line and paste it in place of Hook 2's. If the rest of Hook 2 still reads correctly with no edits, they are the same angle. Real different entry points break when swapped, because each one sets up a different second beat.

### 1.5 Hook mechanics

- **First four words carry a concrete noun or number.** "Eleven people bought it" beats "Nobody really engaged with the launch."
- **Overlay is not a subtitle of the VO.** The overlay carries the number or the stakes; the VO carries the voice. Never print the spoken line on screen word for word.
- **No question hooks with an obvious answer.** "Want more clients?" is dead air.
- **The subject arrives immediately.** No weather, no getting-out-of-bed, no "so I've been thinking a lot about".
- **Screen Share format:** the hook overlay is the number that will be visible on screen, and the screen must actually be able to show it.
- **Comment Response format:** the comment itself has to carry enough context to function as the hook with no setup. If the comment needs explaining, it is not a hook.

### 1.6 Before and after

**Pair A. Three hooks that were one thought (personal trainer, contract: replace cold outbound, proven by inbound consultation requests).**

BEFORE (fails)
> Hook 1: No Clients / Pain Point
> Opening Line: "I was struggling to get new personal training clients."
>
> Hook 2: Empty Schedule / Pain Point
> Opening Line: "My training schedule had way too many empty slots."
>
> Hook 3: Need Clients / Pain Point
> Opening Line: "Finding new clients as a trainer is honestly so hard."

Why it fails: one thought, three paraphrases, all from the Pain Point row. Nothing after the hook has to change, so the three variants test nothing.

AFTER (passes)
> Hook 1: Ninth Cold DM / Pain Point
> Overlay: 9 messages sent. 0 replies.
> Opening Line: "Nine cold DMs before 8am and not one person wrote back."
>
> Hook 2: Wrong Diagnosis / Root Cause
> Overlay: It was never the pitch.
> Opening Line: "I rewrote my outreach message six times. The message was never the problem."
>
> Hook 3: Booked Without Asking / Goal
> Overlay: 6 consults booked. Zero DMs sent.
> Opening Line: "Six consultations on my calendar this month and I did not message a single person to get them."

The fix: pick three different rows of the grid, then give each one its own concrete count so the second beat has to differ.

**Pair B. Claim hook versus moment hook (wedding photographer, contract: booked off-season, proven by weekly inquiries to booked shoots).**

BEFORE (fails)
> Hook 1: Off Season / Timing
> Overlay: Grow your photography business
> Opening Line: "Photographers, if you want to stay booked all year round, you need to be visible where couples are actually looking."

Why it fails: it is a claim plus advice, with no person in it and no number. The overlay is a category slogan. Nothing is at stake in the first three seconds.

AFTER (passes)
> Hook 1: February Silence / Timing
> Overlay: 3 inquiries. All of January.
> Opening Line: "January ended with three inquiries and my second shooter asked if I was still hiring for spring."

The fix: replace the claim with the moment that proves the claim, put the bad number in the overlay, and let a second character make the stakes real.

**Pair C. Delayed subject (founder, contract: launch-day demand, proven by launch-day orders vs. the failed first launch).**

BEFORE (fails)
> Opening Line: "So about a year ago I decided I wanted to start my own thing, and I had this idea I kept coming back to, and eventually I saved enough to get the first run made."

AFTER (passes)
> Overlay: Launch one: 11 orders.
> Opening Line: "My first launch did eleven orders. Four of them were people I am related to."

The fix: start at the worst number, not at the origin story. Backstory, if it earns its place, goes in beat two in one sentence.

## 2. Offer Placement

### 2.1 The rule

The offer enters **inside the product-introduction beat**, in the first 35 to 40 percent of runtime, as a **fact of the story** rather than a pitch. It is repeated once at the end as a `CTA Overlay:` end card. It never appears for the first time in the closing line.

Reason: an offer in the last sentence reads as an ad tacked onto a story. An offer woven into the moment of adoption reads as a decision a real person made, and it de-risks the product before the proof lands, which is where the proof does its work.

### 2.2 The five woven forms

1. **Price fact.** "I grabbed the annual plan while it was fifty percent off."
2. **Trial fact.** "The first week was free, so the only thing I was risking was a Sunday afternoon."
3. **Timing fact.** "I signed up the week before the drop because the launch pricing ended that Friday."
4. **Comparison fact.** "It cost less for the year than the two boosted posts I ran in December."
5. **Plan-choice fact.** "I skipped the top tier and took the middle plan at forty percent off, which was all I needed for one location."

Each form is a *narrated decision*. The exact terms and the deadline live in the `CTA Overlay:` end card, where they can be checked and updated without touching the VO.

### 2.3 Batch dedup

No two scripts in a batch use the same woven form or the same offer sentence. Log it: script 001 price fact, script 002 comparison fact, script 003 trial fact. If two scripts both say "while it was fifty percent off", one of them gets rewritten.

### 2.4 Before and after

**Pair A. Offer parked at the end (bookkeeper, contract: reduce referral dependence, proven by non-referral inquiries per month).**

BEFORE (fails)
> ...and seven of the nine clients I signed last quarter had never heard of me before. If you are tired of waiting on referrals, [PRODUCT] is fifty percent off right now, link in bio.

Why it fails: the offer is a bolt-on. The listener has already decided by then, and the discount arrives with no story attached, so it reads as pressure.

AFTER (passes)
> Beat 3: "A woman in my networking group had been using it for a month, so I took the annual plan on her referral code and paid for the year in one go. It came to less than one client's monthly fee."
>
> ...
>
> CTA Overlay: Annual plan, 50% off through Friday.

The fix: the price becomes part of how she adopted it, benchmarked against a unit she already understands. The end card carries the exact terms.

**Pair B. Offer as a pitch inside the story (mobile groomer, contract: fill weekday gaps, proven by weekday appointments booked).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "That's when I found [PRODUCT], and honestly you guys, their pricing is unbeatable right now, fifty percent off the annual plan, which is such an insane deal for what you get."

Why it fails: the voice breaks. A person recounting their week does not read out a promotion. This is the single fastest way to lose a UGC read.

AFTER (passes)
> "I tried it on a Tuesday, which is my deadest day. The free week meant I could test it on the worst possible day before paying anything, and by that Thursday I had four vans booked in one postcode."
>
> CTA Overlay: Try it free for 7 days, then 50% off annual.

The fix: keep the offer inside the character's reasoning ("test it on the worst possible day before paying anything"). Move the promotional wording to the overlay, where it is allowed to sound like a promotion.

## 3. Product-Introduction Variation

### 3.1 The rule

The way the product enters is **determined by the narrative of that concept**, and **no construction is reused inside a batch**. The banned default is:

> BANNED: "That's where [PRODUCT] came in."

Also banned as a batch-wide crutch: "So I did some research and found [PRODUCT]", "Then I discovered [PRODUCT]", "Enter [PRODUCT]".

### 3.2 The bank (14 constructions)

Pick the one the story earns. Each is shown with an example line you should rewrite, never paste.

1. **Peer recommendation.** Someone credible in the persona's world already uses it. Use when the objection is trust.
   > "The photographer who second-shoots for me had it open on her laptop and asked why I was still doing this manually."

2. **Took it off my to-do list.** The persona was already going to do the work by hand. Use when the pain is time, not skill.
   > "It was item four on a list I had rewritten three times. I gave that item to [PRODUCT] and stopped rewriting the list."

3. **Flipped the order.** They were doing the right things in the wrong sequence. Use for Root Cause hooks.
   > "I had been posting first and looking for the right people second. [PRODUCT] made me do it the other way around."

4. **Second discovery channel.** Their existing channel works but is the only one. Use when the persona is not failing, just fragile.
   > "Referrals were never the problem. Referrals being the only thing was the problem, so I added one more way to get found."

5. **The comparison purchase.** Bought instead of something else. Use with Comparison hooks and comparison offer facts.
   > "I cancelled the boosted posts and put that same budget into [PRODUCT] for the year."

6. **Talked into it.** They resisted and someone pushed. Use for Objection hooks.
   > "My sister set it up for me while I was arguing with her about why it would not work for a service business."

7. **Inherited setup.** They took over someone else's working system. Good for skeptical, non-technical personas.
   > "My old studio manager left me her [PRODUCT] setup like a recipe card, and I just did not touch it."

8. **Deadline forced it.** A date made the decision. Pairs with Timing hooks and timing offer facts.
   > "The drop was nine days out. I was not going to build an audience by hand in nine days."

9. **Free-week experiment.** Framed explicitly as a test with a stop condition. Good for analytical personas.
   > "I gave it one week and one rule: if nobody new booked, I would delete it on Sunday."

10. **Post-mortem decision.** Introduced while reviewing what went wrong. Strong after a failure hook.
   > "I sat down with the launch numbers, wrote down what I actually needed, and it was one thing: people who cared before day one."

11. **Client asked for it.** A customer or lead named the gap. Good for B2B and service personas.
   > "A bride asked why she had to find me through a Facebook group. That question is what made me set this up."

12. **Cheaper than the person I was going to hire.** Replaces a planned hire. Good for cost-of-inaction hooks.
   > "I had a quote from an agency for eleven hundred a month. I tried [PRODUCT] first because I could stop it whenever I wanted."

13. **Already in the workflow.** It plugged into a tool they were opening anyway. Good for low-friction mechanisms.
   > "It sits in the same tab I already open before every shoot, so there was nothing new to remember."

14. **The visible artifact.** The product enters as something on screen, not something narrated. Mandatory option for Screen Share format.
   > "*[Visual Cue: cursor opens the discovery list, 412 accounts, filter set to Local Couples, 60 mile radius]*" with VO: "This is the list I was trying to build in a spreadsheet."

### 3.3 Logging

Before delivery, write down the construction used per script. Two scripts sharing one construction is an instant review failure under Product Introduction and under Differentiation Across Concepts.

### 3.4 Before and after

**Pair A. The banned default, twice in one batch.**

BEFORE (fails)
> Script 001: "I needed a way to reach couples in my area. That's where [PRODUCT] came in."
> Script 002: "I needed a way to reach people before launch day. That's where [PRODUCT] came in."

Why it fails: identical construction, and neither introduction is caused by its own story. Swap them and no one notices, which is precisely the batch failure this skill exists to prevent.

AFTER (passes)
> Script 001 (peer recommendation): "The photographer who second-shoots for me had [PRODUCT] open on her laptop and asked why I was still searching hashtags by hand."
> Script 002 (deadline forced it): "The drop was nine days out. I was not going to hand-build an audience in nine days, so I let [PRODUCT] find the people who already cared about this category."

The fix: derive the introduction from the hook's angle. A Root Cause hook earns "flipped the order"; a Timing hook earns "deadline forced it"; an Objection hook earns "talked into it".

**Pair B. Product arrives too late (past the halfway mark).**

BEFORE (fails)
> Beats 1 to 4 are the founder's origin story, the fabric sourcing, the first sample, the name change. [PRODUCT] first appears at beat 5 of 7.

AFTER (passes)
> Beat 1: "My first launch did eleven orders."
> Beat 2: "Four were relatives. The rest of my list was people who had followed me for the free template."
> Beat 3 (post-mortem decision): "So before launch two, I wrote down the one thing I actually needed, and it was people who cared about this before day one. That is the whole job I handed to [PRODUCT], on the annual plan at half price."

The fix: product by beat 3 of 7 at the latest. Backstory earns one sentence, and only if it sets up the contract's measurable outcome.

## 4. The Proof Ladder

### 4.1 The three rungs

Every script climbs the same ladder, in this order:

1. **Reach metric.** How many relevant people saw or were surfaced. "Four hundred and twelve local accounts", "eleven thousand views from my own city".
2. **Engagement metric.** How many of them did something. "Thirty-one saved it", "nine people replied to the story poll".
3. **Business outcome.** The contract's measurable outcome, in the contract's own unit. Consults booked. Shoots on the calendar. Launch-day orders. Non-referral inquiries.

Rung 3 is not optional and is not substitutable. **The unit in rung 3 must be the unit written in the concept contract.** If the contract says "booked shoots", rung 3 says booked shoots, not "so much more engagement".

### 4.2 Matching proof to the contract

| Contract's measurable outcome | Correct rung 3 | Wrong rung 3 (instant fail) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inbound consultation requests | "Six consults booked, none of them from a DM I sent" | "My engagement went way up" |
| Weekly inquiries to booked shoots | "Nine inquiries that week, four became booked dates in March" | "So many more people were commenting" |
| Launch-day orders vs. failed first launch | "Two hundred and fourteen orders on day one against eleven the first time" | "The launch felt so much bigger" |
| Non-referral inquiries per month | "Seven of nine new clients had never heard my name before" | "My profile got a lot more traffic" |

### 4.3 Number rules

- **Attach a timeframe to every number.** "Nine inquiries" is a shrug. "Nine inquiries in the first week of February" is evidence.
- **Give one baseline.** A number without a before is unreadable. "Three in January, nine in the first week of February."
- **Prefer odd, unrounded figures.** 214 and 31 read as counted. 200 and 30 read as invented.
- **Keep the ladder proportionate.** Reach in the hundreds or low thousands, engagement in the tens, outcome in the single digits or low tens for a service business. A ladder that goes 50,000 to 9,000 to 400 bookings for a solo photographer destroys the whole script.
- **Never state a guarantee or a purchased result.** No "you will get X". No "get 10K followers". Real people, community, reach, discovery.
- **Substantiated first.** Use the brand's real, documented numbers from onboarding where they exist. Invented numbers are the fallback, must be plausible for that persona's business size, and must be flagged to the client under the FTC substantiation note at the top of the document.

### 4.4 What generic proof looks like, and its fix

Generic proof is any rung 3 that could be pasted into a different script in the batch. The tell is the vocabulary: growth, engagement, traction, visibility, momentum, "so many more people". Fix it by replacing the abstraction with the countable thing the persona would actually write in their calendar or their books.

### 4.5 Before and after

**Pair A. Proof that closes the wrong argument (wedding photographer, contract: booked off-season, proven by weekly inquiries to booked shoots).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "Within a couple of weeks my comments were up, my DMs were busier than they had been all year, and my follower count was finally moving again."

Why it fails: three engagement metrics stacked, no business outcome, and the contract promised bookings. This is the exact pattern the client scored 5.5 out of 10.

AFTER (passes)
> "*[Visual Cue: inquiry inbox, February, 9 unread]*
> Four hundred and twelve couples getting married within sixty miles of me got surfaced in the first ten days. Thirty-one of them saved the barn gallery. Nine sent an inquiry that week, and four of those are booked dates in March. February used to be the month I did not open my accounting app."

The fix: full ladder (412 reach, 31 engagement, 9 to 4 outcome), the outcome stated in booked dates because that is the contract's unit, and one line of consequence in the persona's own life.

**Pair B. Proof with no baseline and rounded numbers (founder, contract: launch-day demand vs. the failed first launch).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "The second launch went so much better. We had thousands of people watching and hundreds of orders, it was honestly amazing."

AFTER (passes)
> "*[Visual Cue: orders dashboard, day one, 214]*
> Eleven orders the first time. Two hundred and fourteen on day one this time, and thirty-eight of those came from people who had asked to be told when it dropped. Same product. Same photos. Different room."

The fix: give the failed launch its number so the second one means something, unround the counts, and name the subset that proves the mechanism (pre-launch interest, not luck).

**Pair C. Proof that outruns the persona's business.**

BEFORE (fails)
> Personal trainer script: "Fifty thousand people saw my page, I got nine thousand comments, and I signed four hundred new clients in a month."

AFTER (passes)
> "Eighteen hundred people within a twenty minute drive of my gym got surfaced in two weeks. Forty-one of them followed. Six booked a consult, and four are paying clients as of this morning. I have not sent a cold message since the second week of January."

The fix: scale the ladder to a one-trainer business. Credibility here is worth more than size.

## 5. The Overlay System

### 5.1 The division of labour

| Belongs in `Overlay:` | Belongs in spoken VO |
| --- | --- |
| Numbers, counts, prices, dates | The person's reasoning and feelings |
| The trust or compliance line | The story beats and the decision |
| Category or feature labels | The mechanism explained in their words |
| Exact offer terms and deadlines | The offer as a narrated decision |
| Short reframes that land harder read than said | Anything that needs a human voice to be believed |

**The overlay is never a transcript of the VO.** If a viewer with sound off and a viewer with sound on get the same sentence, one of the two channels is wasted.

### 5.2 The trust and compliance line rule

The trust line exists so the viewer stops worrying about the obvious risk (bots, access, privacy, authenticity). It goes **on screen, not in the mouth**, it appears **at most once per script**, and its **wording changes across the batch**.

```
Overlay: No bots. No password needed.
Overlay: Real accounts only. Nothing automated.
Overlay: Real people. No follow-for-follow.
Overlay: You never hand over your login.
```

Spoken boilerplate is the failure: the same trust sentence read aloud in all six scripts is one of the anti-patterns that fails review on its own. Placement: immediately after the mechanism line, which is exactly where the doubt appears.

### 5.3 Visual cue markers

Syntax, inline, on its own line inside the script body:

```
*[Visual Cue: cursor opens the discovery list, filter set to Local Couples, 60 mile radius, 412 results]*
```

Rules:

- **Name the artifact and the number.** "Shows the app" is not a cue. "Inquiry inbox, February, 9 unread" is a cue.
- **Every proof number gets a cue that could actually be filmed.** If the number cannot be shown, either the number or the format is wrong.
- **Visuals are proof, not decoration.** No cues for mood, none for b-roll that carries no information, none for the persona walking somewhere.
- **Screen Share format:** the on-screen numbers do the persuading, so cue the exact screen state per beat, and the VO explains what the number means rather than reading it out.
- **Comment Response format:** the first cue is the comment itself, quoted in full, and it has to carry enough context to work as the hook with no setup.

### 5.4 Before and after

**Pair A. Trust line spoken in every script.**

BEFORE (fails)
> Script 001 VO: "And the best part is there are no bots, it is all real people, and you never have to give them your password."
> Script 002 VO: "And there are no bots at all, it is all real accounts, and you never give them your password."

Why it fails: identical spoken boilerplate, twice. It reads as script furniture, it eats runtime that the proof beat needs, and it makes both scripts sound like the same performer reading the same brief.

AFTER (passes)
> Script 001 VO: "It builds the list from people who are actually planning a wedding near me, which is the part I could never do by hand."
> Script 001 Overlay: Real accounts only. Nothing automated.
>
> Script 002 VO: "It looks for people already following the three brands my customers compare us to."
> Script 002 Overlay: You never hand over your login.

The fix: mechanism in the voice, trust on the screen, different wording per script, once each.

**Pair B. Overlay duplicating the VO, and decorative cues.**

BEFORE (fails)
> Overlay: I got nine inquiries in one week
> VO: "I got nine inquiries in one week."
> *[Visual Cue: b-roll of her walking through a field with her camera]*

AFTER (passes)
> Overlay: 9 inquiries. First week of February.
> VO: "February is normally the month I stop looking at my inbox."
> *[Visual Cue: inquiry inbox on phone, February, 9 unread, thumb scrolls to show 4 marked Booked]*

The fix: overlay takes the number, VO takes the meaning, and the cue films the evidence instead of the atmosphere.

**Pair C. Screen Share where the screen carries nothing.**

BEFORE (fails)
> *[Visual Cue: screen recording of the dashboard]*
> VO: "You just open it up and it does the work for you, it is really simple."

AFTER (passes)
> *[Visual Cue: screen recording, discovery tab, filter row visible: Location 60mi, Interest Wedding Planning, Window Next 12 Months, result count ticks 0 to 412]*
> VO: "Every filter on that row is a question I used to answer by guessing. The count on the right is how many couples matched all three."

The fix: for Screen Share, cue the specific screen state and let the VO interpret the number rather than narrate the click.

## 6. CTA Rules

### 6.1 The two-move CTA

A CTA does two things, in this order:

1. **Close the specific argument** this concept opened, in the contract's unit. One sentence. It should be readable as the answer to the hook.
2. **Broaden the callout** to the adjacent audience, naming two or three specific personas rather than "everyone".

Then the action, then the end card:

3. **The action**, one clause, concrete. "Link is in the bio."
4. **`CTA Overlay:`** the exact offer terms and the deadline, repeated from the early woven placement.

### 6.2 What fails

- **Broadening first.** "If you're a business owner of any kind..." before the specific argument is closed throws away the whole narrative.
- **Closing an argument the script never made.** If the script proved booked shoots, do not close on "stop worrying about your follower count".
- **New claims in the CTA.** The close cannot introduce a benefit, a number, or a feature that the body did not earn.
- **Naked action.** "Link in bio" with no close is not a CTA.
- **The offer's first appearance.** If the offer shows up here for the first time, the script fails Offer Placement regardless of how good the close is.
- **Broadening into nothing.** "Anyone who wants to grow" is not a broadened callout. Three named personas is.

### 6.3 Before and after

**Pair A. Generic close (personal trainer, contract: replace cold outbound, proven by inbound consultation requests).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "So if you want to grow your business and get more clients, definitely check out [PRODUCT], link in my bio. Fifty percent off right now."

Why it fails: closes nothing, broadens to everyone, and the offer appears here for the first time.

AFTER (passes)
> "I have not sent a cold message since the second week of January, and my consults are still filling. That is the whole change.
> If you are a trainer, a physio, or anyone running a coaching practice off cold DMs, this is the part you can stop doing by hand.
> Link is in my bio.
>
> CTA Overlay: 50% off the annual plan. Ends Friday."

The fix: close in the contract's unit (consults, no cold messages), broaden to three named adjacent personas, single clean action, terms on the end card.

**Pair B. Close that contradicts the hook (wedding photographer, Timing hook, off-season contract).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "At the end of the day it is all about building a real community, and [PRODUCT] helps you do that. Go check them out."

AFTER (passes)
> "February used to be the month I quietly panicked. This year February booked March.
> If you shoot weddings, run a venue, or do any work with a season that goes quiet, the quiet part is the part worth fixing.
> Tap the link in my bio.
>
> CTA Overlay: Annual plan, 50% off, through Friday."

The fix: the close answers the exact hook ("February Silence") in the contract's unit (booked dates), and the broadening stays inside the seasonal-business logic instead of jumping to "everyone".

**Pair C. New claim in the close (bookkeeper, non-referral inquiries contract).**

BEFORE (fails)
> "And it works for literally any industry, plus it saves you like ten hours a week, so if you want that, link below."

AFTER (passes)
> "Seven of my last nine clients had never heard my name before they found me. My referral network is still there, it is just not the only thing holding the business up.
> If you are a bookkeeper, a fractional CFO, or any solo operator whose pipeline is one person's goodwill away from empty, that is the risk this removes.
> Link is below.
>
> CTA Overlay: 50% off annual. Offer ends Friday."

The fix: no new claims, close restates the earned proof, and the broadening names the shared risk rather than a shared industry.

## 7. Self-check before handing to the Creative Strategist

Run this on every script. Any "no" is a rewrite, not a note.

1. Can I state this script's contract in one line, and does the hook open it?
2. Do the three hooks come from three different rows of the angle grid, and is each labelled `Hook N: Situation / Angle Type`?
3. Would swapping hook 1's opening line into hook 2 break hook 2? (It must.)
4. Does the offer appear inside the product-introduction beat, in the first 35 to 40 percent, as a narrated decision?
5. Is the product introduced by beat 3 of 7 at the latest, using a construction from the bank that this story actually earned?
6. Is that construction unused by every other script in the batch? Is the offer phrasing unique too?
7. Does the proof climb reach -> engagement -> business outcome, with a timeframe and one baseline?
8. Is rung 3 stated in the contract's exact unit, not in growth or engagement language?
9. Is the ladder proportionate to this persona's business size?
10. Is the trust line an overlay, once, worded differently from the other scripts in the batch?
11. Does every proof number have a filmable visual cue that names the artifact and the number?
12. Does the CTA close the specific argument first, then broaden to two or three named personas, then act, then repeat the offer on the end card?
